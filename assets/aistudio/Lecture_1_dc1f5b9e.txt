📊 Lecture 1: Frequency Distributions – The Complete Guide

Welcome to the world of Statistics! In this lecture, we learn how to turn raw, messy data into clear, organized information that helps us make better decisions.

1. What is Statistics?

Statistics is the science of collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing, and interpreting data to assist in making more effective decisions.

· Example: Suppose the inflation rate for the year was 0.7\%. By using statistics, we can compare this to past years. Is it higher? Lower? Is there a trend? Is there a relationship between interest rates and government bonds? Statistics helps us answer these questions.

Two Branches of Statistics:

1. Descriptive Statistics: Methods of organizing, summarizing, and presenting data in an informative way. (Think: Describing what happened).
2. Inferential Statistics: Methods used to estimate a property of a population on the basis of a sample. (Think: Inferring or predicting based on a small group).

Key Terms:

· Population: The entire set of individuals or objects of interest (or all measurements from them).
  · Example: All cars in a parking lot.
· Sample: A portion or part of the population of interest.
  · Example: Just 10 cars selected from that parking lot. (See the image on Page 4: the sample is a small box drawn from the big population box).

2. Types of Variables

A variable is a characteristic being measured. There are two main types:

A. Qualitative Variable

· Definition: An object or individual is observed and recorded as a non-numeric characteristic or attribute.
· Examples: Gender, State of birth, Eye color, Brand of PC, Marital status, Hair color.

B. Quantitative Variable

· Definition: A variable that is reported numerically.
· Examples: Balance in your checking account, Life of a car battery, Number of people employed by a company.
· Quantitative variables are split into two sub-types:
  1. Discrete Variables: Typically the result of counting. Values have "gaps" between them (you can't have half a person).
     · Examples: Number of bedrooms in a house (1, 2, 3, 4...), Number of students in a class (326, 421...), Children in a family, Strokes on a golf hole.
  2. Continuous Variables: Usually the result of measuring something. Can assume any value within a specific range (fractions and decimals are allowed).
     · Examples: Duration of flights (5.25 hours), Grade point average (3.258), Amount of income tax paid, Weight of a student, Yearly rainfall in Tampa, FL.

3. Frequency Tables (Organizing Qualitative Data)

A Frequency Table groups qualitative data into mutually exclusive (no overlap) and collectively exhaustive (covers everything) classes, showing the number of observations in each class.

· Example (Page 8):
  Location Number of Cars
  Kane 52
  Olean 40
  Sheffield 45
  Tionesta 43
  Total 180

Relative Frequency

This shows the proportion or percentage of the total. It is found by dividing the frequency by the total.

· Formula: \text{Relative Frequency} = \frac{\text{Class Frequency}}{\text{Total Frequency}}
· Example: Kane = 52 / 180 = 0.289 (or 28.9%).
· Note: The sum of all relative frequencies must always equal 1.000 (or 100%).

4. Graphic Presentation

How do we visualize this data? Two main charts:

A. Bar Chart

· Shows qualitative classes on the horizontal axis (X) and frequencies on the vertical axis (Y).
· The heights of the bars are proportional to the frequencies.
· Example (Page 10): A bar chart showing the number of vehicles sold in Kane, Tionesta, Sheffield, and Olean.

B. Pie Chart
· A circle that shows the proportion or percentage each class represents of the total.
· Example (Page 11): A pie chart showing Sedan (40%), SUV (50%), Compact (25%), Truck (85%), Hybrid (95%). (Note: These are cumulative percentages or angled slices; a standard pie chart divides 100%, but this image shows the cumulative nature of the categories).

5. Frequency Distributions (Organizing Quantitative Data)

When you have a lot of numbers, you group them into classes.

Steps to Construct a Frequency Distribution:

1. Decide on the number of classes. (Usually between 5 and 15).
2. Determine the class interval (width).
3. Set the individual class limits (the top and bottom numbers for each group).
4. Tally the data into the classes and count the observations.

Step-by-Step Example: Wachesaw Manufacturing (Pages 16-18)

Here is the data of units produced over 16 days:
27, 27, 27, 28, 27, 25, 25, 28, 26, 28, 26, 28, 31, 30, 26, 26

The Problem: Construct a frequency distribution table and a histogram using 6 classes.

The Solution:

1. Find the Range: \text{Max} - \text{Min} = 31 - 25 = 6.
2. Find Class Width: \text{Range} / \text{Number of Classes} = 6 / 6 = 1. So, each class will have a width of 1.
3. Tally and Count:

   · Data sorted: 25, 25, 26, 26, 26, 26, 27, 27, 27, 27, 28, 28, 28, 28, 30, 31.
   Let's build the table based on the solution on Page 18:
   · Class 25 – 26: Frequency = 4 (Relative Freq: 4/16 = 0.25)
   · Class 26 – 27: Frequency = 4 (Relative Freq: 0.25)
   · Class 27 – 28: Frequency = 4 (Relative Freq: 0.25)
   · Class 28 – 29: Frequency = 2 (Relative Freq: 0.125)
   · Class 29 – 30: Frequency = 1 (Relative Freq: 0.0625)

The Histogram

A Histogram is a graph of a frequency distribution for quantitative data.

· The horizontal axis shows the class limits (25-26, 26-27, etc.).
· The vertical axis shows the frequencies.
· Key Difference from a Bar Chart: In a histogram, the bars touch each other because the data is continuous (Page 14 shows a histogram for the Profit data, where the bars are connected).

Important Terms for Quantitative Tables (Page 18):

· Relative Error: The proportion of the total (e.g., 4/16 = 0.25).
· Cumulative Frequency: The running total of frequencies (e.g., 4, 8, 12, 14, 15, 16).
