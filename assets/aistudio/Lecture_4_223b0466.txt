Lecture 4: Confidence Interval — Full Explanation

1. Point Estimation vs. Interval Estimation

Let's start with the big picture. In statistics, we have two worlds:

· The population — the entire group we care about. Its numerical summaries are called parameters (e.g., population mean \mu, population variance \sigma^2).
· The sample — the subset we actually observe. Its numerical summaries are called statistics (e.g., sample mean \overline{X}, sample variance s^2).

We use statistics to estimate parameters. A single statistic used for this purpose is a point estimate — for example, the sample mean \overline{X} or the sample proportion. It's called a "point" because it's one single number.

The problem: a point estimate is almost never exactly equal to the true parameter. So how far off might it be? That's exactly what a confidence interval answers — it adds a margin of error around the point estimate, indicating how far off the true value is likely to be.

Simple analogy: If someone asks your height, saying "175 cm" is a point estimate. Saying "I'm between 173 and 177 cm, and I'm 95% sure of that" is an interval estimate — much more informative!

---

2. Definition of a Confidence Interval

A confidence interval for a population parameter is an interval constructed from sample data that is expected to contain the true value of the parameter with a specified level of confidence.

For the population mean \mu, a two-sided confidence interval generally has the form:

\text{Point Estimate} \pm \text{Margin of Error}

So the interval is symmetric around the point estimate. The margin of error is the "buffer" we add and subtract.

---

3. Definition of Confidence Level

The confidence level is the proportion of all possible random samples for which the confidence interval constructed by a given procedure will contain the true population parameter.

A confidence level of 100(1-\alpha)\% corresponds to a significance level \alpha, where 0 < \alpha < 1.

Common confidence levels:

· 90% → \alpha = 0.10
· 95% → \alpha = 0.05
· 99% → \alpha = 0.01

⚠️ Important interpretation: 95% confidence does NOT mean "there is a 95% probability that \mu lies in this interval." The parameter \mu is fixed; the interval is random. It means: if we repeated the sampling procedure many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would capture \mu. Think of it as a long-run success rate of the method.

---

4. Case 1: Large Sample Size (n \geq 30)

Let X_1, \ldots, X_n be a large random sample (n \geq 30) from a population with mean \mu and standard deviation \sigma. By the Central Limit Theorem, \overline{X} is approximately normally distributed.

Then a level 100(1-\alpha)\% confidence interval for \mu is:

\overline{X} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma_{\overline{X}} \quad (5.4)

where

\sigma_{\overline{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}

is the standard error of the mean.

When \sigma is unknown (which is the usual case in practice), we replace it with the sample standard deviation s:

\overline{X} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}

Why z_{\alpha/2}? Because for a two-sided interval, we split the remaining area \alpha equally between the two tails — \alpha/2 in each tail. The critical value z_{\alpha/2} is the z-score that cuts off area \alpha/2 in the right-hand tail.

---

5. The Famous Critical Values (Memorize These!)

Confidence Level Critical Value z_{\alpha/2} Interval
68% 1 \overline{X} \pm 1\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
90% 1.645 \overline{X} \pm 1.645\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
95% 1.96 \overline{X} \pm 1.96\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
99% 2.58 \overline{X} \pm 2.58\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
99.7% 3 \overline{X} \pm 3\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}

The 68–95–99.7 rule (empirical rule) is the intuition behind the first, third, and fifth rows: 68% of a normal distribution lies within 1 standard deviation, 95% within 1.96, and 99.7% within 3.

Notice that this interval is centered at the sample mean \overline{X}, not at \mu — and in this particular draw, it happens to contain \mu. If we repeated the experiment, 95% of such random intervals would contain \mu.

7. Worked Example 1: Battery Amperages (85% CI)

Problem: The sample mean and standard deviation for the cold cranking amperages of 100 batteries are \overline{X} = 185.5 and s = 5.0. Find an 85% confidence interval for the mean amperage.

Solution — step by step:

Step 1 — Point estimate:

\overline{X} = 185.5

Step 2 — Standard error:

\sigma_{\overline{X}} \approx \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{5.0}{\sqrt{100}} = \frac{5.0}{10} = 0.5

Step 3 — Critical value: Set 1 - \alpha = 0.85, so:

\alpha = 0.15, \qquad \frac{\alpha}{2} = 0.075

We need z_{0.075}, the z-score that cuts off 7.5\% of the area in the right-hand tail. From the z-table:

z_{0.075} = 1.44

Step 4 — Margin of error:

(1.44)(0.5) = 0.72

Step 5 — Confidence interval:

185.5 \pm 0.72 = (184.78,\; 186.22)

Interpretation: We are 85% confident that the true mean amperage of all such batteries lies between 184.78 and 186.22.

8. Worked Example 2: Normal Distribution with Known Variance (95% CI)

Problem: Determine a 95% confidence interval for the mean \mu of a normal distribution with variance \sigma^2 = 9, sample size n = 100, and sample mean \bar{x} = 5.

Given:

· \bar{x} = 5
· \sigma^2 = 9 \implies \sigma = 3
· n = 100
· 1 - \alpha = 0.95 \implies \alpha = 0.05 \implies \alpha/2 = 0.025

Critical value from the standard normal table:

z_{0.025} = 1.96

The formula:

\bar{x} - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \leq \mu \leq \bar{x} + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}

Substituting the values:

5 - 1.96 \cdot \frac{3}{10} \leq \mu \leq 5 + 1.96 \cdot \frac{3}{10}

5 - 0.588 \leq \mu \leq 5 + 0.588

4.412 \leq \mu \leq 5.588

Answer:

(4.412,\; 5.588)

Notice the margin of error is 1.96 \times 0.3 = 0.588. Since the population variance was known here, we used \sigma = 3 directly, not s.

---

9. Two Crucial Notes: Width and Sample Size

Note I — The width (margin of error) of a confidence interval:

\pm\, z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}

What affects the width?

· Higher confidence level → larger z_{\alpha/2} → wider interval.
· Larger \sigma (more variability in the population) → wider interval.
· Larger n → smaller \sigma/\sqrt{n} → narrower interval (more precise).

There is always a trade-off between confidence and precision: to be more confident, you must accept a wider (less precise) interval — unless you increase the sample size.

Note II — Sample size needed for a target width:

To construct a level 100(1-\alpha)\% confidence interval of width \pm w:

n = \frac{z_{\alpha/2}^{2}\,\sigma^{2}}{w^{2}} \quad (5.5)

Why? Set the margin of error equal to w:

z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = w \;\Longrightarrow\; \sqrt{n} = \frac{z_{\alpha/2}\sigma}{w} \;\Longrightarrow\; n = \frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2\sigma^2}{w^2}

⚠️ Always round n UP to the next integer — you can't sample a fraction of a battery, and rounding down would give you less precision than required.

---

10. Worked Example 3: Determining Sample Size (99% CI)

Problem: In the amperage example, the sample standard deviation from 100 batteries was s = 5.0\text{ A}. How many batteries must be sampled to obtain a 99% confidence interval of width \pm 1.0\text{ A}?

Solution:

Step 1 — Confidence level: 1 - \alpha = 0.99, so \alpha = 0.01, and:

z_{\alpha/2} = z_{0.005} = 2.58

Step 2 — Estimate \sigma with s = 5.0.

Step 3 — Apply Equation (5.5) with w = 1.0:

n = \frac{(2.58)^2 (5.0)^2}{(1.0)^2} = \frac{6.6564 \times 25}{1} = 166.41

Step 4 — Round up:

n \approx 167
Answer: We need to sample 167 batteries. Notice this is larger than the original 100 — to be more confident (99% vs. 85%) with a tight \pm 1.0 A margin, we need more data.

11. Key Takeaways & Summary

Concept Formula / Value
Point estimate of \mu \overline{X}
Standard error \sigma_{\overline{X}} = \sigma/\sqrt{n} \approx s/\sqrt{n}
CI (large sample) \overline{X} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
Margin of error z_{\alpha/2}\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
Required sample size n = \dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}^2\sigma^2}{w^2}, rounded up

Important vocabulary to remember (in English):

· Statistic vs. Parameter
· Point estimate
· Interval estimate / Confidence interval
· Margin of error
· Confidence level (100(1-\alpha)\%)
· Significance level (\alpha)
· Standard error of the mean (\sigma_{\overline{X}})
· Critical value (z_{\alpha/2})
· Two-sided (two-tailed) interval

The intuition in one sentence: A confidence interval is a "net" we cast around our sample mean; the confidence level tells us how often that net catches the true parameter \mu across repeated samples — and we control the net's width by choosing the confidence level and the sample size.
