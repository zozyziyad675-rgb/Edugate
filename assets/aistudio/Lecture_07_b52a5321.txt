Lecture 07: Determinants and Square Systems (Inverse & Cramer's Rule)

Welcome to Lecture 7! In the previous lecture, you learned a golden rule: A matrix is invertible if and only if its determinant is non-zero.

In this lecture, we are going to connect determinants to solving Square Systems of Linear Equations. We will explore two powerful methods: the Inverse Method and Cramer's Rule.

---

1. Theorem 1: The Golden Equivalencies

This theorem is the foundation of everything in this lecture. For an n \times n matrix A, the following four statements are equivalent (meaning if one is true, they are all true; if one is false, they are all false):

· (a) A is nonsingular (it has an inverse, A^{-1}).
· (b) \det(A) \neq 0 (the determinant is not zero).
· (c) The system Ax = b has a unique solution for every n \times 1 column matrix b. The solution is exactly x = A^{-1}b.
· (d) The system Ax = 0 (homogeneous) has only the trivial solution (where all variables equal 0).

Why is this amazing? It means that if you are asked to solve a system Ax = b, the very first thing you should do is calculate \det(A).

· If \det(A) \neq 0, Boom! You immediately know there is exactly one unique solution, and you can use the Inverse method or Cramer's Rule.
· If \det(A) = 0, the inverse method and Cramer's rule cannot be used. You must use Gaussian elimination to find out if it has infinitely many solutions or no solution.

Example 1 (From the slides)

Suppose you are given two systems of equations.

· System 1: You calculate the determinant of its coefficient matrix A, and you find |A| \neq 0.
· System 2: You calculate the determinant of its coefficient matrix B, and you find |B| = 0.

Conclusion: According to Theorem 1, only System 1 has a unique solution. You can solve System 1 using Gauss-Jordan, the Inverse Method, or Cramer's Rule. However, for System 2, you cannot use the Inverse Method or Cramer's Rule; you must use elimination to see if it has infinite solutions or no solution.

---

2. Cramer's Rule (Theorem 2)

Cramer's Rule gives us a direct formula to find the solution of a system Ax = b using only determinants.

The Rule:
Let A be an n \times n nonsingular matrix (\det(A) \neq 0), and let b be a column vector. Let A_k be the matrix obtained by replacing the k-th column of A by the vector b.

The unique solution x is found by:

x_k = \frac{\det(A_k)}{\det(A)}, \quad \text{for } k = 1, 2, \dots, n

How the Proof works (Briefly):
Since x = A^{-1}b = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \text{adj}(A)b, if you expand the adjoint formula, the numerator turns out to be exactly the determinant of the modified matrix A_k.

Let's make this concrete with examples!

---

Example 2: Cramer's Rule for a 2 \times 2 System

Solve the following system:

4x_1 - 2x_2 = 10

3x_1 - 5x_2 = 11

Step 1: Find the determinant of the coefficient matrix A.

|A| = \begin{vmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ 3 & -5 \end{vmatrix} = (4 \times -5) - (-2 \times 3) = -20 + 6 = -14

Because |A| \neq 0, we know a unique solution exists.

Step 2: Apply Cramer's Rule.
To find x_1, we replace the first column of A with the constants (10, 11)^T:

x_1 = \frac{|A_1|}{|A|} = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} 10 & -2 \\ 11 & -5 \end{vmatrix}}{-14} = \frac{(10 \times -5) - (-2 \times 11)}{-14} = \frac{-50 + 22}{-14} = \frac{-28}{-14} = 2

To find x_2, we replace the second column of A with the constants (10, 11)^T:

x_2 = \frac{|A_2|}{|A|} = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} 4 & 10 \\ 3 & 11 \end{vmatrix}}{-14} = \frac{(4 \times 11) - (10 \times 3)}{-14} = \frac{44 - 30}{-14} = \frac{14}{-14} = -1

Final Answer: x_1 = 2, x_2 = -1.

Example 3: Cramer's Rule for a 3 \times 3 System

Solve the following system:

x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3 = 5

2x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3 = 6

x_1 + 2x_2 + 3x_3 = 9

Step 1: Find |A|. (The slide assumes you calculate this, and the result is |A| = -4).
Because |A| \neq 0, there is a unique solution.

Step 2: Apply Cramer's Rule.
The slide gives the final results based on the determinants of A_1, A_2, and A_3:

· x_1 = \frac{|A_1|}{|A|} = \frac{-4}{-4} = 1
· x_2 = \frac{|A_2|}{|A|} = \frac{-4}{-4} = 1
· x_3 = \frac{|A_3|}{|A|} = \frac{-8}{-4} = 2

(Tip: If you want to practice, try calculating |A_1| by replacing the first column with [5, 6, 9]^T and check if you get -4!)

3. Example 4: The Grand Comparison (Solving with Three Methods)

In this example, we have a 3 \times 3 system, and we are given that |A| = \det(A) = 2 \neq 0. 
Because |A| \neq 0, we immediately know:

1. A is nonsingular (invertible).
2. The system Ax = b has a unique solution.
3. Ax = 0 has only the trivial solution.

The slide shows how to solve it using two methods (and leaves the third as an activity).

Method 1: The Inverse Method
(Note: The exact matrix calculations for the inverse are on Page 4 of the original PDF, though not explicitly typed out in the text. But the concept is to find A^{-1} and multiply it by b, x = A^{-1}b.)

Method 2: Cramer's Rule
Look at the beautiful, clean calculation on Page 5 of the PDF!
The original matrix A is:

A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}

They calculate the four determinants:

· \Delta = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 2 (This is |A|)
· \alpha = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 1 (Replaced column 1 with b)
· \beta = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = -1 (Replaced column 2 with b)
· \gamma = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 3 (Replaced column 3 with b)

The solution is then:

· x_1 = \frac{\alpha}{\Delta} = \frac{1}{2}
· x_2 = \frac{\beta}{\Delta} = \frac{-1}{2}
· x_3 = \frac{\gamma}{\Delta} = \frac{3}{2}

Method 3: Gauss-Jordan
The slide leaves this as an activity ("try yourself"). Hint: You can set up the augmented matrix [A | b] and use row operations to reduce it to the identity matrix [I | x]. You should get the exact same answers: x_1 = 1/2, x_2 = -1/2, x_3 = 3/2.

Summary Checklist for Exams

When facing a square system Ax = b:

1. Calculate \det(A) first.
2. If \det(A) \neq 0, you are guaranteed a unique solution.
3. Choose your weapon:
   · Cramer's Rule: x_k = \frac{\det(A_k)}{\det(A)}. Great for small systems (2x2 or 3x3).
   · Inverse Method: x = A^{-1}b.
   · Gauss-Jordan: Augmented matrix [A | b] \rightarrow [I | x].
4. If \det(A) = 0, do not use Cramer's Rule or the Inverse Method. Use Gauss-Jordan elimination to find if the system is inconsistent (no solution) or dependent (infinitely many solutions).
